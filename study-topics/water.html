Drinking Water

Bellevue vs Newark — tap water for drinking, boiling & soup · assessed 2026-09-03

01The question

For two homes — ignoring cost, and using no filter pitcher, under-sink filter or reverse-osmosis unit — is it necessary to buy bottled mineral water for daily drinking, boiling water and making soup?

Bellevue, Washington
Apartment built in 2025 · municipal water (Cedar & Tolt supply)
Newark, Delaware
Single-family house built in 1970 · known copper plumbing · assumed on City of Newark municipal water, not a private well

“Nordic standard” is not a single regulation. This note references the EU Drinking Water Directive 2020/2184 and Sweden’s stricter precautionary PFAS4 guideline separately, alongside US compliance limits.

Why

  • The municipal lead result is low.
  • 1970 copper plumbing may still have legacy lead solder or brass fixtures.
  • Reported PFAS concentrations exceed Sweden’s strict PFAS4 target.
  • Boiling does not remove lead or PFAS.

In short

  • Bellevue: use cold tap water directly for boiling and soup — no need to buy mineral water.
  • Newark: by current US compliance results alone, the tap water is usable; if you hold to the Swedish-style strict PFAS4 standard and refuse filtration, prefer bottled water with a complete test report for drinking and water-absorbing cooking.
  • Newark’s issue is not hard water — it is PFAS plus the uncertainty of end-of-line fittings in an old house.
  • “Mineral water” does not automatically mean safer. Bottled water needs PFAS, lead and other test data for the finished product.

Soft water is not a health deficiency — dietary calcium and magnesium come mainly from food. Highly mineralized water is not necessarily healthier either; it scales more and changes the taste of soup, tea and coffee.

05What to do without a filter

Bellevue

Water: cold municipal tap water

Procedure

  • After long stagnation, run the tap until the water is noticeably cold.
  • Start from cold tap water, then heat it.
  • Use it for drinking and all cooking.

Bottled water’s role: optional, for personal taste only.

Newark

Under the US compliance view

  • Cold tap water can be used for drinking and cooking.
  • After sitting overnight, flush until the water is noticeably cold.
  • Recommended: one first-draw lead test at the house tap.

Under the strict Swedish PFAS4 precautionary view

  • Use verified bottled water for direct drinking, tea, coffee, soup, congee, rice and any food that retains its cooking water.
  • Keep using tap water for washing produce and dishes, cleaning, bathing, and any cooking where the water is fully discarded.
  • Boiling does not remove PFAS or lead, and evaporation may slightly concentrate non-volatile substances.

06Choosing bottled water

“Natural”, “imported”, “Nordic source” or “mineral water” is not proof of safety. Check the most recent report for the finished bottled product first:

ItemPreferred specification
PFAS4 sum<4 ppt, preferably non-detect

SodiumLow
NitrateDocumented and low
Report scopeThe finished bottled product, not just the source
Packaging (cost ignored)Glass

For long-term boiling and soup, don’t blindly pick highly mineralized water above 150–200 mg/L hardness — it scales more readily and can carry a noticeable mineral taste.

07Caveats

Where “POPs” stops applying

POPs (persistent organic pollutants) is a broad category that includes, among others:

  • Some PFAS, e.g. PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS
  • PCBs
  • Dioxins and furans
  • DDT and its metabolites
  • Organochlorine pesticides such as aldrin, dieldrin, chlordane

Bellevue has not published any figure that could be called a “total POPs concentration”. The public data only supports:

  1. The 29 PFAS tested were extremely low or not detected.
  2. The utility states only a few of ~200 tested compounds were detected.
  3. This does not allow the claim that all Stockholm Convention POPs are at absolute zero.
  4. Different compounds have different toxicities and standards — don’t simply add all the numbers together for a health judgment.

Key limitations

  1. This note uses public water-system data; it is not a lab test of a specific tap.
  2. The Newark conclusion assumes the house is served by the City of Newark municipal water system; if the supplier differs or it is a private well, the analysis must be redone.
  3. “Non-detect” means below a given method’s detection or reporting capability — not absolute zero.
  4. PFAS reports may use different sampling dates, detection limits and statistics; they cannot be blended into a precise long-term average exposure.
  5. Household lead risk depends on stagnation time, faucet, solder, valves and the private-side service line.
  6. Water notices and standards change; check the latest annual water quality report and any temporary drinking-water advisories before relying on this.
