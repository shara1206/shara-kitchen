Milk Labels Decoded

What organic, grassfed, pasture raised and the rest actually guarantee

A milk carton answers four separate questions, and the labels for one of them tell you nothing about the other three. Organic is about feed and drugs. Grassfed is about diet composition. Pasture raised is about time outdoors — and has no federal definition at all. Pasteurized / homogenized / A2 are about what happened after the milk left the cow.

The Label Table

Group Label Core standard & definition Why it’s worth paying for Watch out / limitations
Feed & drugs USDA Organic Certified organic Core standard & definition 100% organic feed (no GMOs, no synthetic pesticides); no antibiotics and no rBST, ever; and — unlike organic chicken — a real grazing requirement: a grazing season of at least 120 days a year, during which pasture must supply at least 30% of dry matter intake (7 CFR 205.237–205.240). Why it’s worth paying for The only label that covers feed, drugs and pasture at once. It is federally enforced, annually audited, and it is the one seal that already contains rBST-free and non-GMO, so those add nothing on top of it. Watch out / limitations 30% is an average, and the other 70% can be organic grain. Organic is not grassfed. On a large western dry-lot dairy the herd can hit 120 days and 30% and still spend most of the year in a barn or corral — which is exactly what the Cornucopia scorecard was built to separate.
Feed & drugs 100% Grassfed (certified) AGA / Certified Grassfed by AGW / PCO Core standard & definition Grass and forage for life after weaning — all grain is banned, in every form (whole, ground, silage, distillers). AGA dairy also requires grazed pasture to supply ≥60% of dry matter intake, a grazing season of ≥150 days, no CAFO confinement, and US-born-and-raised animals. Why it’s worth paying for The strongest guarantee that the cow was actually on grass. It is also the only label that reliably buys you the nutrition difference — higher omega-3 and CLA come from forage, not from the organic seal. Watch out / limitations Grassfed does not imply organic. The pasture can be sprayed and fertilized unless the carton also carries the USDA Organic seal. Certified Grassfed by AGW is the exception that bundles welfare in — it requires AWA certification first.
Feed & drugs “Grass-Fed” (no seal, no 100%) Bare marketing claim Core standard & definition Essentially nothing. USDA withdrew its grass fed marketing claim standard in January 2016; since then the words on a carton are backed only by whatever affidavit the company filed. Every ruminant eats some grass, so the claim is nearly always technically true. Why it’s worth paying for Occasionally it is shorthand for a genuinely pasture-based small dairy that has not paid for certification — worth checking the brand’s own website, but not worth a premium on its own. Watch out / limitations The single most misleading word in the dairy case. “Grass-fed” without 100% and without a certifier’s seal usually means grass plus grain. Look for the word 100% and a logo, or ignore it.
Living conditions Pasture Raised / Pasture Grazed Raised on pasture Core standard & definition Intended to mean the herd lives outdoors on real pasture for most of the year, eating growing grass. There is no federal definition for dairy and no required audit — the phrase means exactly what the brand decides it means. Why it’s worth paying for When it is backed by a seal, it is the best welfare signal on the shelf. Time on grass is what drives both cow welfare and the flavor and color difference you can actually taste in the cream. Watch out / limitations Alone, it is marketing. Treat it as a claim, not a certification: it only counts next to Animal Welfare Approved, Certified Humane, Regenerative Organic or the USDA Organic seal, which at least carries the 120-day / 30% floor.
Living conditions Animal Welfare Approved (AWA) A Greener World Core standard & definition Independently audited, and the only major US welfare seal that requires continuous pasture or range access for the whole herd. Applies per farm, not per plant, so it cannot be bought by a large aggregator pooling anonymous milk. Why it’s worth paying for The highest-bar welfare seal in the dairy case. If a carton has AWA, the “pasture raised” wording next to it is verified rather than claimed. Watch out / limitations Small supply and mostly regional — rare on a Seattle shelf outside co-ops and farmers markets. It also says nothing about feed being organic unless the organic seal is there too.
Living conditions Certified Humane HFAC third-party audit Core standard & definition Third-party audited standards for space, bedding, handling and stockmanship, with routine antibiotic growth promoters banned. For dairy it sets barn and management requirements — it does not require cows to be raised on pasture. Why it’s worth paying for A real audit with real records, and far above the conventional industry baseline. On a large national brand it is often the only independent verification you will get. Watch out / limitations Certified Humane is not a pasture guarantee. A fully indoor, well-managed dairy can carry the seal, and some painful procedures are still permitted. Read it as “audited”, not as “outdoors”.
Living conditions Regenerative Organic Certified ROC — Bronze / Silver / Gold Core standard & definition USDA Organic as the floor, then soil health, animal welfare and farmworker fairness on top. All tiers require continuous open access to pasture; the grassfed share rises by tier — Bronze >50%, Silver >75%, Gold 100% grassfed. Tail docking is prohibited. Why it’s worth paying for The one seal that bundles all four questions at once — feed, drugs, pasture and welfare — and the only one with a public tier system, so Gold is a single-word shorthand for the best carton on the shelf. Watch out / limitations Check the tier, not just the logo. Bronze only promises >50% grassfed, which is well below a 100% Grassfed seal. Very few dairy brands carry ROC at all, and it is the most expensive milk in the case.
Feed & drugs rBST-free / No artificial growth hormones rBST / rBGH not used Core standard & definition An affidavit from the farms that no recombinant bovine growth hormone was injected. FDA requires the claim to be paired with a context statement — the familiar small print that no significant difference has been shown between milk from rBST-treated and untreated cows. Why it’s worth paying for Free, essentially. Most US processors dropped rBST years ago under retailer pressure, so the claim now costs nothing extra — take it when it is there. Watch out / limitations Never pay a premium for it. It is a single-issue, self-declared claim, it says nothing about antibiotics, feed or pasture, and USDA Organic already prohibits rBST outright.
Feed & drugs Non-GMO Project Verified Butterfly seal — feed only Core standard & definition Third-party verification that the herd’s feed — mainly corn, soy and alfalfa — is not genetically engineered, with segregation and testing through the supply chain. Milk itself contains no transgenic DNA either way. Why it’s worth paying for Useful on a non-organic carton, where it is the only thing verifying anything about the feed at all. Watch out / limitations Redundant on organic milk. Organic already bans GMO feed, so the butterfly adds nothing there — and non-GMO feed can still be sprayed, so it is the weaker of the two.
Processing Pasteurized (HTST) / Vat Pasteurized Standard & low-temp pasteurization Core standard & definition HTST is 161°F for 15 seconds — the US default. Vat (batch, “low-temp”) is 145°F for 30 minutes, gentler on flavor and on the proteins that matter for cheesemaking and yogurt. Why it’s worth paying for This is the milk that still tastes like milk. Vat-pasteurized is the one to buy for yogurt, paneer or ricotta — and the difference in a plain glass is obvious. Watch out / limitations Short shelf life — roughly two to three weeks, and the date is real. Vat-pasteurized milk is mostly small-dairy and regional, so it costs more per gallon.
Processing Ultra-Pasteurized (UP) / UHT Super-heated; UHT is shelf-stable Core standard & definition UP is 280°F for 2 seconds — still refrigerated, but 45–90 days of shelf life. UHT is the same heat into aseptic packaging, so it sits on a warm shelf until opened. Nearly all national organic milk is UP. Why it’s worth paying for It is what makes organic milk shippable nationwide, and it is genuinely convenient — a carton that survives a month in the fridge and a box that survives the pantry. Watch out / limitations The cooked flavor and the failed yogurt both come from here. UP milk tastes faintly caramelized and sets poorly into yogurt or fresh cheese because the whey proteins are already denatured.
Processing Cream-Top / Non-Homogenized Cream separates to the top Core standard & definition Homogenization forces milk through a fine valve to break fat globules small enough to stay suspended. Skip it and the cream floats — that is the entire difference. It is unrelated to pasteurization, organic or grassfed. Why it’s worth paying for You can skim the cream off the top. Free heavy cream for coffee or a scone batch, richer mouthfeel, and a visible cream line is a fair proxy for a small dairy that did not over-process the milk. Watch out / limitations You have to shake the bottle every time, and the claim that homogenized fat is harmful has no good evidence behind it — buy cream-top for the cream and the texture, not for a health reason.
Processing Raw / Unpasteurized Retail-legal in Washington Core standard & definition No heat treatment at all. Interstate sale for human consumption is banned by FDA (21 CFR 1240.61), so it is a state-by-state matter — Washington licenses retail raw milk, with WSDA inspection, testing and a warning label required. Why it’s worth paying for The fullest flavor of any milk you can buy, and a licensed WA dairy is a fully traceable, individually tested supply chain rather than a pooled one. Watch out / limitations Real pathogen risk — testing reduces it, it does not remove it. Not for children, pregnancy or anyone immunocompromised, and the shelf life is days. Nutritionally the claimed advantages over pasteurized milk are small.
Breed & protein A2 / A2A2 Beta-casein variant Core standard & definition Milk from cows genotyped as carrying only the A2 form of beta-casein, so it lacks the A1 variant that digestion turns into BCM-7. Verified by the brand’s own herd DNA testing — there is no federal standard or seal behind the letters. Why it’s worth paying for Worth one trial carton if regular milk leaves you bloated but a lactose-free carton did not fix it — that pattern points at the protein rather than the sugar. Watch out / limitations A2 is not lactose-free — the sugar is identical, so it does nothing for lactose intolerance. The comfort evidence is thin and mostly industry-funded, and A2 milk says nothing about feed, pasture or processing.
Breed & protein Jersey / Guernsey Single-breed herd Core standard & definition A breed claim, not a certification. Jersey and Guernsey milk runs roughly 4.5–5% fat against about 3.7% for Holstein, with more protein and more beta-carotene — which is why the cream looks yellow. Why it’s worth paying for The best milk for cooking with. Higher fat and casein mean richer custard and panna cotta, better cheese yield, and thicker yogurt without adding cream. Watch out / limitations Breed is not welfare and not feed — a Jersey herd can be as confined as any other. Jerseys skew A2, but only a genotyped herd can print A2, so do not read one label into the other.

USDA Organic is federally regulated and audited. AGA / AGW / PCO 100% Grassfed, Certified Humane, Animal Welfare Approved, Regenerative Organic and Non-GMO Project are third-party certifications. Pasture Raised, bare “grass-fed”, rBST-free and A2 are company claims with no independent seal behind them.

Organic ≠ Grassfed ≠ Pasture Raised

These three get used as if they were increasing grades of the same thing. They are not — they are three separate axes, and a carton can be strong on one and empty on the other two.

  • Organic guarantees clean feed and no drugs, plus a grazing floor of 120 days and 30% of intake from pasture. It does not guarantee a grain-free diet or a life outdoors.
  • 100% Grassfed guarantees the diet — no grain, ever — and with AGA, 60% of intake from grazed pasture over a 150-day season. It does not guarantee the pasture is organic.
  • Pasture Raised guarantees nothing by itself. It becomes real only when AWA, Certified Humane or ROC is printed beside it.
  • The nutrition difference you read about — omega-3, CLA, beta-carotene — tracks forage, not the organic seal. If that is what you are buying, the label that delivers it is 100% Grassfed, not Organic.
  • Organic + 100% Grassfed on the same carton is the combination that covers both axes — and Regenerative Organic Gold is that pair plus audited welfare, in one seal.

Heat Treatment at a Glance

Method Time & temperature Shelf life Best for
Raw No heat Days Flavor; accept the risk
Vat / batch (“low-temp”) 145°F / 30 min ~2 weeks Yogurt, paneer, ricotta, drinking
HTST (standard) 161°F / 15 sec 2–3 weeks Everyday milk; yogurt still works
Ultra-pasteurized (UP) 280°F / 2 sec, refrigerated 45–90 days Coffee, cereal, baking; poor for yogurt
UHT / aseptic Same heat, sterile packaging Months, unrefrigerated Pantry backup, travel

Pasteurization is a food-safety step, not a quality grade — but the higher the heat, the more cooked the flavor and the worse the milk sets. Almost all nationally distributed organic milk is ultra-pasteurized, which is why organic milk from a big brand often tastes flatter than conventional milk from a local dairy.

Quick Takeaways

  • Read the carton in this order: certification seals first (Organic, 100% Grassfed, AWA / Certified Humane / ROC), then the heat treatment, then everything else. The unsealed words are the last thing to look at, not the first.
  • “Grass-fed” without 100% and without a certifier logo is the one claim to ignore outright. The federal standard behind it was withdrawn in 2016.
  • rBST-free and Non-GMO add nothing to an organic carton. Organic already prohibits both, so a stack of small seals is not a stack of guarantees.
  • If you make yogurt or fresh cheese, the heat treatment matters more than any of the seals. Vat-pasteurized or plain HTST sets; ultra-pasteurized fights you.
  • The best realistic carton in a Seattle store: USDA Organic + 100% Grassfed, cream-top if you can find it, and vat-pasteurized or HTST rather than UP — then check the brand against the Cornucopia scorecard.
  • A2 and breed labels are about digestion and cooking, not farming. Judge the farming from the other three groups regardless of what the front of the bottle says.
