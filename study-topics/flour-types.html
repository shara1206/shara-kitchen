Flour Types & Protein: When to Use What

May 21, 2026 · Flour · Reference

TL;DR

The single most important number on a bag of flour is protein content. Protein turns into gluten when wet, and gluten gives structure. More protein = chewier, stretchier (bread). Less protein = more tender (cake). Wheat variety is a sub-question of that: "hard" wheat is high-protein, "soft" wheat is low-protein. Everything else — bleach, organic, stone-ground — matters less than picking the right protein level.

The protein principle

Wheat protein + water + agitation = gluten. Gluten is the network that traps gas bubbles and gives chew. So:

  • You want structure (bread that holds its shape, chewy bagels): high-protein flour, long mixing.
  • You want tenderness (cake, biscuits, pie crust): low-protein flour, minimal mixing.
  • Same flour, different result: Pancakes get tough if you over-mix AP because too much gluten develops. Mix until just combined.

Notes on each type

All-purpose

The middle child. Brands vary in protein: King Arthur AP is 11.7%, which is on the high end. Gold Medal AP is ~10.5%. White Lily AP is ~9% (essentially soft-wheat pastry flour, which is why it's so beloved in Southern biscuit recipes). So "AP" isn't one thing — the brand matters more than you'd think.

Bread flour

Higher protein than AP, ~12–14%. The extra gluten gives stretchier dough that holds its rise. If a yeast-bread recipe specifies bread flour, swapping in AP gives a denser crumb — usually still good, just different.

Cake & pastry flour

Made from soft wheat. Cake flour is often chlorinated (bleached), which weakens proteins further and helps absorb more sugar and fat — that's how cake recipes get such a tender, fine crumb. Pastry flour is in between cake and AP — perfect for pie crust and biscuits.

00 flour

An Italian grading by fineness of grind (00 = finest, then 0, 1, 2). 00 isn't a protein level by itself — "00 pizza" is high-protein, "00 cake" is lower. The finer grind absorbs water faster and feels silky. For Neapolitan pizza, look for "00 Tipo Pizza" or "00 Forte" specifically.

Whole wheat

Contains the bran and germ, not just the endosperm. More fiber, more nutrients, more flavor — but the sharp bran particles physically cut gluten strands, so even though whole wheat tests at ~13% protein, its functional gluten is lower. That's why 100% whole wheat bread is denser than white. Common trick: substitute 25–50% whole wheat into a white recipe for flavor and nutrition without crushing the rise.

What about bleached vs unbleached?

Bleached flour is chemically treated (usually chlorine or benzoyl peroxide) to whiten it and weaken proteins. It's good for cakes, biscuits, and pancakes — the weakened protein keeps things tender. Unbleached flour is whitened naturally by aging, has slightly stronger gluten, and is preferred for bread. Many bakers default to unbleached for everything as a flavor preference; it's a small difference.

Stone-ground vs roller-milled: Stone-ground retains slightly more germ and bran (more flavor, faster spoilage). Roller-milled is the industrial standard (cleaner, longer shelf life). For whole wheat, stone-ground tastes noticeably better; for white flour, the difference is minimal.

Quick reference

  • One flour to own: King Arthur Unbleached AP (11.7%) — strong enough for most breads, tender enough for cookies/muffins.
  • Add a second flour: Bread flour for serious yeast baking, OR cake flour for serious cakes.
  • Storage: White flour: ~1 year in pantry. Whole wheat: 3 months pantry, up to 6 months fridge/freezer (the germ has oil that goes rancid).
