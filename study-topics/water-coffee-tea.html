Coffee

  • Carbon-filtered tap water works. Its alkalinity is below the SCA target of 40, so cups lean bright and a little thin.
  • Rounder without adding anything: 1 part Fiji + 2 parts filtered tap (alkalinity ≈58, hardness ≈52).
  • Skip Kirkland purified water for coffee — it is even softer than the tap.

Tea

  • Filtered tap water is close to ideal for green, white and oolong tea: soft and low in bicarbonate.
  • Remove the chlorine first — carbon filter, or leave the water uncovered overnight.

  • 1 part Fiji + 2 parts Kirkland lands near the SCA alkalinity target with nothing added.
  • Deer Park on its own: test its KH first (section 05) — its springs vary too much to guess.

Tea

  • Use one soft bottled water alone: Deer Park if it tests soft; Kirkland also works for green tea.

  • Why the tap is out: see the PFAS data on the Drinking Water page.

In one line

  • Coffee wants a little buffer (alkalinity ≈40); tea wants as little bicarbonate as possible, but not zero minerals. One water rarely suits both — so coffee gets a blend, and tea gets the softest clean water on hand.

02Why coffee and tea want different water

The same two numbers — alkalinity and hardness — push the two drinks in opposite directions.

Coffee: SCAA brewing water standard (2009). Tea, general: Tea Association of the USA, as quoted by Barista Hustle. Green / white: common Japanese-tea retailer guidance (e.g. Kettl).

Coffee: alkalinity is the dial

  • Bicarbonate neutralizes part of coffee’s acids. Near 0 → sharp and sour; around 40 → balanced; above about 80 → flat and muted.
  • Calcium and magnesium carry body and sweetness; very soft water tastes thin.
  • Seattle tap has some buffer (20–30); purified water has almost none (≈10 or less).

Tea: bicarbonate is the problem

  • Bicarbonate keeps the brew slightly alkaline, which oxidizes tea polyphenols — darker color and lower antioxidant activity (Beijing tap-water study).
  • Harder water gives more white precipitate, a darker infusion, and less EGC, EGCG and vitamin C; amino acids, caffeine and sugars barely change (Japanese green-tea study).
  • Calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate together form the film on the surface (tea scum).
  • Zero minerals is not best either: in the same Japanese study, tasters preferred green tea made with a soft mineral water over distilled water.

Black tea and oolong tolerate more minerals than green or white tea. Some fermented dark teas even taste less sour in harder water.

04Blend two ready-made waters (coffee)

No salts, no drops — just two waters you can buy. For tea, use the softer water alone.

The blending base: which bottle is FIJI

FIJI Water is bottled artesian water from the Fiji Islands in the South Pacific, common in US grocery stores.

How to spot it

  • Square, clear bottle with a blue cap.
  • The label art is printed on the back, so a pink hibiscus flower shows through the water.
  • The front reads FIJI · Natural Artesian Water. Common sizes: 500 mL (16.9 fl oz) and 1 L.

Why it works for blending: the label lists bicarbonate 152 mg/L (calcium 18, magnesium 15, silica 93). That is enough buffer for one part to lift two parts of a softer water into the coffee range.

Blend value = (value₁ × parts₁ + value₂ × parts₂) ÷ total parts Works for alkalinity and hardness alike. Mix by volume. Example Fiji 1 + Kirkland 2 Alkalinity (125 + 2 × 10) ÷ 3 = ≈48 Hardness (107 + 2 × 7) ÷ 3 = ≈40

05Deer Park: test once, then decide

Deer Park mixes many springs, so its report range tells you nothing about your bottle. One KH test settles it.

How to test

  1. Buy an aquarium KH (carbonate hardness) drop-test kit.
  2. Follow the kit: count drops until the color changes. On most kits one drop ≈ 1 dKH.
  3. Convert: dKH × 17.9 = alkalinity in mg/L as CaCO₃.
  4. Retest when you buy a new case — the spring may have changed.

No kit? Call the number on the label (1-800-288-8281) and ask for the analysis of the spring printed on your bottle.

06Practical notes

Chlorine

  • Seattle disinfects with free chlorine, which hurts the aroma of both coffee and tea.
  • Fix: an activated-carbon filter, or leave the water uncovered overnight. Boiling a few minutes longer also drives it off.
  • Some pitcher cartridges also contain ion-exchange resin, which strips bicarbonate — fine for tea, but it makes coffee water even softer.

Measuring

  • A TDS pen reads the total only; it cannot tell bicarbonate from calcium or sodium.
  • For brewing, KH (alkalinity) is the one number worth testing.

Delaware: safety before taste

  • Choose bottled water by its finished-product PFAS and lead report first, minerals second — criteria on the Drinking Water page.
  • Deer Park’s 2025 report lists PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS and PFBS as not detected (per Complete Water Guide’s summary).
  • Kirkland and Fiji PFAS data: not checked here — look up their latest reports before relying on them.
  • Boiling does not remove PFAS.

07Caveats

  1. Bottled water varies by source and batch; recheck the label when you switch brands or cases.
  2. Kirkland’s numbers come from one Southern California plant’s report posted online; Washington and Delaware bottles may come from other plants.
  3. SPU figures are system-level, not a test of your tap; Cedar and Tolt differ slightly.
  4. Blend values are averages by volume from rounded label figures — good to about ±5 mg/L.
  5. SCA targets are for specialty coffee, and taste is personal. Tea-water research is small, and there is no consensus on a single best tea water.
